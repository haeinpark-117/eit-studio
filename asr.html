Speech recognition

Why Whisper,
and with which settings.

Speech recognition was, for a long time, the reason automated elicited imitation did not work. The choice of model, and the way it is configured, is therefore part of the measurement rather than an implementation detail.

Why Whisper large-v3-turbo

Whisper is an open speech recognition model released by OpenAI (Radford et al., 2023): it takes an audio recording and returns a transcription of what it hears. EIT Studio runs it on every response and scores that transcription against the target sentence.

Choosing a speech recognition system for this task is not the same as choosing one for dictation. In an elicited imitation task the participant hears a sentence and repeats it as closely as possible, and the score is the distance between the sentence presented and the words actually produced. The deviations are the measurement, so a system that tidies them away removes the very thing being measured. That rules out the usual method of raising accuracy on learner speech: earlier systems reached usable accuracy on learner responses only through tuning that often included forced alignment, a procedure that constrains the output to the words of the prompt.

A system that can only return the target sentence will return the target sentence, and every deviation disappears with it. Whisper is accurate enough on accented, disfluent speech to be used without being told what to expect, which is what this task requires. Four properties Radford et al. (2023) report are what make that possible.

Accuracy

Error rates on standard benchmarks that approach human transcription, and that are competitive with fully supervised systems.

Trained at scale

Trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual, multitask supervision rather than on a single curated corpus of read speech.

Robustness

Trained to generalize across recording conditions, accents, and speech styles rather than to one variety — which is what makes accented learner speech, and recordings made on participants' own phones and laptops, usable.

No adaptation needed

Used zero-shot, without dataset-specific fine-tuning — and, as above, without forced alignment, which is what makes the scores meaningful.

Whisper is released in several sizes, of which large-v3 is the largest. The turbo variant is that same model with its decoder layers reduced — the half that interprets the audio is left intact and only the half that writes the words out is made smaller — which, per its model card, costs only minor quality while running several times faster. That is what lets it run inside a browser rather than on rented hardware. Reaching Whisper through a commercial API is not expensive, at USD 0.006 per minute of audio for OpenAI's whisper-1 endpoint, but it requires an account, a token, and a script, and the recordings leave the researcher's machine. The turbo weights are openly available, so the same model runs from Python or here in the browser at no cost and with nothing uploaded. The smaller whisper-small model remains selectable for checking that a file set is configured correctly, but it should not be used for scoring.

How it performs on learner speech

What Whisper is asked to transcribe matters more than any other single factor: O'Connor Russell et al. (2024) found it at 3.12 % word error rate (WER) on read audiobook speech but 19.4 % on telephone conversation and 26.7 % on videoconferencing speech.

Elicited imitation responses sit at the favorable end of that range — short single sentences, recorded under controlled conditions, with no overlapping talk. On read speech from learners specifically, McGuire (2026) compared five current systems on 2,400 recordings from the L2-ARCTIC corpus and found Whisper large-v3 lowest in match error rate (.054), though performance varied by first language.

Accuracy is not the whole story, however. Whisper has been reported to transcribe toward an intended reading, regularizing morphosyntax and omitting disfluencies rather than reporting them verbatim (Shen et al., 2026; Zusag et al., 2024). How far this occurs in a given set of responses is an empirical question, which is why a human-verified subsample is advisable where the deviations are themselves the measurement.

Treating WER as a measure of L2 proficiency

Three checks bear on that claim, and all three belong in a methods section. The first concerns the transcription, the second the scoring, and the third the score itself. Reporting only the first supports only the transcription.

01

Fidelity of the transcription

Compare
The error rates computed from a subsample you transcribe by hand against those computed from the automatic transcription of the same responses.
Establishes
That the automatic transcription is faithful enough to carry the metric — and nothing about the score beyond that.
Where
The subsample may be transcribed in the scoring tool itself: correcting a row records the hand transcription alongside the automatic one, and both word error rates appear in the export, so the comparison is computed from a single file.
02

Agreement with established human scoring

Compare
The automatic scores against the same responses scored by human raters using an established scoring method for the task.
Establishes
Convergent evidence that the two ways of scoring order participants alike. Agreement on total scores does not guarantee it item by item.
03

Relationship with an independent proficiency measure

Compare
The automatic scores against a standardized test, a C-test, or another established instrument administered separately.
Establishes
Criterion-related evidence that the scores relate to proficiency as measured independently of this task.

If the model changes

Speech recognition models improve, and this tool will adopt better ones when the evidence supports it. Because that would alter the scores, the model name and version are printed beneath every result table and included in the exported files, and should be reported in any study. Should the default model change, the previous version will remain selectable so that an analysis can be reproduced exactly as it was originally run.

The recognition settings used in EIT Studio

These are fixed rather than exposed as options, because varying them would make scores incomparable across studies. Reporting them is what allows a reader to reproduce the procedure.

Model onnx-community/whisper-large-v3-turbo, run in the browser through transformers.js. Encoder in fp16 and decoder quantised to q4 on WebGPU; WASM at q8 where WebGPU is unavailable.
Decoding Greedy search — do_sample = false, num_beams = 1, temperature = 0. The task calls for verbatim repetition of what the participant produced, so the decoding must be deterministic: the same recording yields the same transcription every time. Sampling would introduce run-to-run variation into the score itself, and beam search would let a globally more fluent hypothesis override a locally accurate one.
No prompt, no bias The model is never given the target sentence, and no vocabulary or initial prompt is supplied. This is the single most important setting: constraining recognition toward the expected words would make the transcription converge on the target and erase the deviations that constitute the measurement.
No cross-item context Each recording is decoded independently, with no conditioning on previously decoded text. One item's transcription therefore cannot influence the next, and the order in which files are processed does not affect the result.
Language Fixed to English (language = "en", task = "transcribe"). Leaving language detection to the model risks it identifying a strongly accented response as the speaker's first language: Ballier et al. (2024) found intermediate French learners of English identified as speaking French in 80 % of cases. Fixing the language removes that failure mode. The translate task is never used.
Audio Resampled to 16 kHz mono, the rate Whisper operates at, with a cap of 128 generated tokens — ample for a single-sentence response and a guard against runaway repetition.
Confidence Not produced by the model directly. A logits processor records the distribution at each decoding step, and the reported figure is the mean log probability of the tokens actually chosen, shown both as a probability and in raw form. It is comparable within a session but not across checkpoints or decoding strategies.
