The evidence base

Why elicited imitation is worth using.

Elicited imitation tasks are among the most extensively validated efficient measures of second language proficiency available to researchers. This page summarizes what the evidence supports.

Why an efficient proficiency measure matters

Second language proficiency is a covariate in nearly every SLA study, yet it is rarely measured with an independent instrument. Across 500 studies published in major SLA journals between 2012 and 2019, over 90 % assessed proficiency in some form but only 42 % used an independent measure; institutional status remained the dominant method (Park et al., 2022).

The obstacle is rarely conviction but cost. In a survey of 111 SLA researchers, endorsement of recommended assessment standards coexisted with practical constraints — time, accessibility, ease of administration — that drove actual practice; reduced-redundancy measures were rated favorably on both validity and practicality (Park et al., 2025).

Elicited imitation is the paradigm case of a reduced-redundancy measure. Learners hear a sentence and repeat it as accurately as they can. Because successful repetition requires parsing and reconstructing form and meaning under processing constraints rather than echoing a surface string (Bley-Vroman & Chaudron, 1994; Vinther, 2002), the ability to reproduce such stimuli has been used as a proxy for global oral proficiency.

It discriminates, and it converges

Yan et al. (2016) meta-analyzed 24 independent effect sizes from 1,089 participants and found a strong ability to discriminate across proficiency levels (Hedges' g = 1.34), with construct, sentence length, and scoring method as significant moderators. Kostromitina and Plonsky (2022), pooling 60 correlations from 46 reports, report an average correlation with other proficiency measures of r = .66, again with longer stimuli associated with stronger correlations. Design decisions matter; the method itself is not fragile.

It withstands operational test development

Davis and Norris (2021) documented the EIT built for the TOEFL Essentials test, across prototyping (419 participants) and piloting (701 participants) in multiple countries.

The graded pattern is itself evidence: correlations are highest with other speaking tasks and lowest with reading, which is what a measure of oral proficiency should produce. The task also extends beyond the prototypical L2 learner — Solon et al. (2022) applied Rasch modeling to a 30-item Spanish EIT with heritage language learners, obtaining excellent reliability and strong correlations with the DELE.

Where EIT Studio fits

EIT Studio reduces the burden on both sides of an elicited imitation study: administering the task, and scoring what it produces.

Administration runs in the browser. A sentence set is delivered on the schedule the original design specifies — stimulus, fixed pause, ring tone, and a response window scaled to sentence length — so that every participant is given the same timing rather than whatever pace the researcher reads at (Ortega et al., 2002; Wu et al., 2022). Each response is written out as its own file, so the recordings need no separate splitting step before they are scored.

Scoring runs in the same place. Transcription and word error rate computation are handled locally, and the normalization rules are explicit, editable, and exportable, so that the exact configuration can be archived with a study. Scoring method is among the moderators identified in the meta-analytic literature (Yan et al., 2016); making it a stated setting rather than an incidental one is part of the point.

Automated scoring has been examined, and it looks promising

Independent studies have compared automatically produced scores against human scoring across different recognition systems, metrics, and languages.

The metric was adapted to the language in each case: Isbell et al. (2023) scored Korean with morpheme- and letter-based Levenshtein distances rather than whitespace-delimited word error rate. Automated scoring appears to generalize across languages, but the specific metric does not transfer unexamined. What these procedures ask of the researcher is technical — platform accounts, scripting, code to adapt — which is what EIT Studio removes (more on that here).

Automatic transcription does not replace human verification. Reporting a human-verified subsample remains good practice, and the scoring rules used should be deposited with the study materials.

The agreement figures above were obtained for English. Comparable evidence for other languages is not yet available, which is why EIT Studio is currently scoped to English elicited imitation tasks.
