The evidence base

Why elicited imitation is worth using.

Elicited imitation tasks are among the most extensively validated efficient measures of second language proficiency available to researchers. This page summarizes what the evidence supports.

The problem it addresses

Second language proficiency is a covariate in nearly every SLA study, yet it is rarely measured with an independent instrument. In a synthesis of 500 studies published in major SLA journals between 2012 and 2019, Park et al. (2022) found that although over 90 % of studies assessed and reported proficiency in some form, only 42 % used an independent measure to do so; institutional status remained the dominant method.

The obstacle is rarely conviction. It is cost. Standardized proficiency tests are expensive, lengthy, and unavailable for many languages, and locally developed measures are difficult to compare across studies. A survey of 111 SLA researchers (Park et al., 2025) found that researchers broadly endorse recommended assessment standards but that practical constraints — time, accessibility, ease of administration — drive what they actually do, producing a consistent belief–practice gap. In that survey, reduced-redundancy measures were rated favorably on both validity and practicality.

Elicited imitation is the paradigm case of a reduced-redundancy measure. Learners hear a sentence and repeat it as accurately as they can. Because successful repetition requires parsing and reconstructing form and meaning under processing constraints rather than echoing a surface string (Bley-Vroman & Chaudron, 1994; Vinther, 2002), the ability to reproduce such stimuli has been used as a proxy for global oral proficiency.

It distinguishes proficiency levels

Yan et al. (2016) reviewed 76 theoretical and empirical studies published between 1970 and 2014 and meta-analyzed 24 independent effect sizes from 1,089 participants across 21 studies. EI tasks showed a strong ability to discriminate between speakers across proficiency levels (Hedges' g = 1.34). Construct, sentence length, and scoring method emerged as significant moderators of that sensitivity, which indicates that design decisions matter rather than that the method is fragile.

It converges with other proficiency measures

Kostromitina and Plonsky (2022) retrieved 46 reports contributing 60 independent correlations, weighted and averaged. The result portrays the EIT as a generally consistent measure of L2 proficiency (r = .66). Stimulus length was positively associated with stronger correlations, a finding that converges with the moderator analysis in Yan et al. and that informs contemporary EIT design recommendations.

It withstands operational test development

Davis and Norris (2021) documented the design and validation of the EIT built for the TOEFL Essentials test across a prototyping phase (419 participants) and a pilot phase (701 participants) in multiple countries. The reported psychometrics are strong for a task of this length.

The graded pattern is itself evidence. Correlations are highest with other speaking tasks and lowest with reading, which is what a measure of oral proficiency should produce.

It extends beyond the prototypical L2 learner

Solon et al. (2022) applied Rasch modeling to a 30-item Spanish EIT administered to heritage language learners. Scores demonstrated excellent reliability and correlated strongly with an independent proficiency measure (the DELE), supporting the task's utility with this population.

Where this tool fits

Two of the moderators identified in the meta-analytic literature — scoring method and consistency of procedure — are matters of implementation rather than of the instrument itself. They are also where researcher time is actually spent.

Manual transcription and scoring of oral EIT responses is the principal practical cost of administering the task, and variation in scoring procedure is a documented source of between-study inconsistency. EIT Studio automates the transcription and word error rate computation, and makes the normalization rules explicit, editable, and exportable, so that the exact scoring configuration can be archived with a study and reused by others.

Automated scoring has been examined, and it holds up

Several independent studies have now compared automatically produced elicited imitation scores against human scoring, using different speech recognition systems, different scoring metrics, and different languages. The convergence across them is what makes the approach credible.

Note that the metric was adapted to the language in each case. Isbell et al. (2023) scored Korean transcriptions with morpheme- and letter-based Levenshtein distances rather than whitespace-delimited word error rate, precisely because Korean orthography does not support the latter interpretation. Automated scoring generalizes across languages; the specific metric does not transfer unexamined.

What has been missing is access

The procedures above are convincing, but they are not equally available to everyone. Isbell et al. (2023) required accounts with commercial ASR platforms and scripted comparison of transcription metrics; Kim et al. (2024) is explicitly a programming tutorial, implemented in Java and drawing on commercial natural language processing services. A researcher who is persuaded by this evidence but does not write code cannot act on it.

EIT Studio exists to close that gap. It implements the same logic — automatic transcription followed by an edit-distance scoring metric — as a web page requiring no installation, no account, no API key, and no programming. The methodological decisions that would otherwise be buried in a script are surfaced as settings that can be inspected, edited, and exported.

Automatic transcription does not replace human verification. Reporting a human-verified subsample alongside automatically scored data remains good practice, and the scoring rules used should be reported or deposited with the study materials.

Note also that the agreement figures above were obtained for English. Comparable evidence for other languages is not yet available, which is why this tool is currently scoped to English elicited imitation tasks.

Why Whisper large-v3-turbo

Speech recognition was, for a long time, the reason automated elicited imitation did not work. The choice of model is therefore not incidental.

The obstacle was L2 speech itself

As McGuire and Larson-Hall (2025) recount, speech recognition systems were built to recognize clear native speech, and their accuracy dropped substantially on non-native or accented speech — the principal bottleneck preventing reliable automated elicited imitation. The sustained effort to automate the task between 2008 and 2012 used the open-source Sphinx system, which struggled with English L2 learner responses and required considerable fine-tuning to reach even modest accuracy.

Those adjustments included forced alignment: constraining the recognizer to the words of the prompt. For elicited imitation this is self-defeating. A recognizer that can only output the target sentence will tend to report the target sentence, and the very deviations that constitute the measurement disappear. An automated procedure for this task needs a recognizer that transcribes what was said without being told what was expected.

What Whisper changed

McGuire and Larson-Hall (2025), drawing on Radford et al. (2022), identify four properties that made Whisper suitable where earlier systems were not.

AccuracyError rates approaching those of human transcription, closer to human level than any previous system
L2 and accented speechTrained on multilingual data, so non-native and heavily accented speech is handled without special provision
Recording qualityHigh tolerance for background noise, so recordings made on participants' own phones and laptops remain usable
No adaptation neededNeither fine-tuning nor forced alignment is required — which, as above, is what makes the scores meaningful

How it performs on learner speech

The claim can be checked directly. McGuire (2026) compared five current speech recognition systems on 2,400 recordings of read English from the L2-ARCTIC corpus — 24 speakers across six first-language backgrounds (Arabic, Chinese, Hindi, Korean, Spanish, Vietnamese), with four US English speakers as a control — scoring each with match error rate.

Whisper and AssemblyAI performed significantly better than the remaining systems, with no significant difference between the two (Friedman χ² = 471.93, df = 4, p < .001). Whisper is thus at the front of the field on exactly the kind of data an elicited imitation study collects, and it is the only one of the two that is open and can be run without an account. Error rates for all five systems were substantially lower than those reported for the systems used in earlier work.

Performance did vary by first language. With Whisper, US English controls produced a mean MER of .007, Hindi .021, Korean .033, Spanish .046, Arabic .047, Chinese .053, and Vietnamese .124. The ordering was consistent across all five systems, which suggests it reflects the availability of similarly accented training data rather than anything peculiar to Whisper. It is worth knowing which end of that range your participants are likely to fall on.

Why the turbo variant

Whisper large-v3-turbo is large-v3 with its decoder layers reduced, retaining the same encoder and, per its model card, only minor quality degradation while running several times faster. That difference is what allows the model to run inside a browser on an ordinary laptop rather than on a rented GPU. McGuire and Larson-Hall (2025) processed their 900 responses through a commercial API on a remote T4 GPU, at a cost of about USD 0.23 and requiring a Python script and an API token; the turbo variant removes both the cost and the token, at the price of running on the researcher's own hardware. The smaller whisper-small model remains selectable for quickly checking that a file set is configured correctly, but it should not be used for scoring.

What to check in your own data

Two properties of Whisper are worth knowing when the speakers are learners. First, performance is not uniform across accents: Graham and Roll (2024) found higher error rates for non-native than native accents, with proficiency negatively predicting error. Second, Whisper transcribes toward an intended reading rather than a verbatim one, and tends to omit fillers, repetitions, and other disfluencies (Zusag et al., 2024). For most elicited imitation scoring this is harmless or even helpful, since hesitation markers are not part of the target sentence; but if disfluency is itself of interest, the transcription should not be treated as verbatim. Items where the participant produced nothing are also worth inspecting directly. These are reasons to verify a subsample by hand, not reasons to avoid the method.

If the model changes

Speech recognition models improve, and this tool will adopt better ones when the evidence supports it. Because that would alter the scores, the model name and version are printed beneath every result table and included in the exported files, and should be reported in any study. Should the default model change, the previous version will remain selectable so that an analysis can be reproduced exactly as it was originally run.
